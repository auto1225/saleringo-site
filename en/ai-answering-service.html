Buyer's guide

Answering service, AI receptionist, or something that finishes the job?

Three different products get sold with almost the same sentence. They do very different things with the customer who just called you, and the difference shows up in your revenue, not in the demo. Here is how to tell them apart before you sign anything.

New here? Start with the two-step tour — about a minute  →

Written by Saleringo — who sell one of these three. We've tried to write the guide we'd want to read if we were buying, and we say plainly which parts favour us.

Start with the only question that matters

Forget features for a moment. A customer calls your business at 9 PM about a job worth $2,000. Nobody is there. Ask each vendor one thing: walk me through everything that happens to that person between 9 PM and 9 AM. The answer separates the three categories instantly.

An answering service ends the story with a message. An AI receptionist ends it with a booking. An AI front office ends it with a booking, a priced quote, a customer record, and a number on your dashboard the next morning.

What each one actually does

1 · The traditional answering service

A real human, usually in a call center, answers under your business name and takes a message. They are polite, they work nights, and they stop the call going to voicemail. They cannot know your prices, see your calendar, or answer the question that would have closed the sale. Their product is the message — so tomorrow morning, your team still calls every one of those people back.

Typical cost runs a few hundred dollars a month, often billed per minute or per call, so a busy month costs more than a quiet one. Worth it if your only goal is that a human voice answers.

2 · The AI receptionist

A newer category, and a real improvement. It answers instantly, in any language, never has a bad day, and — this is the meaningful jump — it can usually book into a connected calendar. It answers common questions from information you gave it, and hands the hard ones to a person.

Most of them stop the moment after the booking. You get a transcript, a calendar entry and a notification — but no quote built from your own price list, no customer record that survives past this week, no pipeline of who is still deciding, no month-end report on which channel earned the money. Cost is typically $50–300 a month plus usage.

3 · The AI front office — answering plus the CRM behind it (what Saleringo is — the rest of this site says “AI receptionist” because that is the word people search)

Saleringo is in this category, so read this paragraph with suspicion. The argument: answering was never the expensive part. The expensive part is what fails to happen afterwards — the quote that takes four days, the follow-up nobody remembers, the customer whose details live on a sticky note, the month-end where you know your revenue but not where it came from.

So the conversation is the input to a system, not the output. The AI collects what a booking needs, holds the slot, prices the job from your price list and discount rules, and sends the estimate the same hour.

During the call it writes the customer record — summary, what they wanted, how they sounded, what to do next. Then it moves them through pipeline stages, chases the follow-up on schedule, and rolls it all into one dashboard.

Which one should you actually buy?

Genuinely, not all of you should buy the third one.

Buy an answering service if… the value of a call is low, the questions are simple, and what you mostly need is for a human voice to exist at 2 AM. If your customers just want to know whether you're open, you do not need any of this.
Buy a plain AI receptionist if… you already run a CRM or practice-management system that your team genuinely uses every day, and the only hole in your operation is the moment of pickup. Feed your existing system. Don't buy a second one you'll ignore.
Buy an AI front office if… your jobs are quoted rather than fixed-price, your sale takes more than one conversation, and you honestly cannot say today which channel brought you last month's revenue. That combination is where the transcript stops being enough — and it describes most clinics, venues, home-services companies, academies and boutique stays we talk to.
Buy nothing yet if… you don't know how many inquiries you currently miss. Pull your call log first. If the answer is "hardly any", every option on this page is a solution to a problem you don't have, and you should keep your money.

Five questions to ask any vendor on this list

  • 1Walk me through everything that happens to a 9 PM caller by 9 AM. Make them narrate it, step by step, without slides.
  • 2Can it quote from my price list, with my options and my discount rules — and what happens when a request falls outside them?
  • 3What does it do when it doesn't know? "It says it doesn't know and gets a person" is the right answer. "It figures something out" is not.
  • 4Show me the report I'd open on a Monday. If there isn't one, you are buying a phone feature, not a business system.
  • 5Does it connect to the software I already run — today, or on a roadmap? Ask for the honest word. Ours is here, including the rows where the answer is "not yet".

Where this guide is biased, stated plainly

We sell the third category, so of course the table has a column that wins. Weigh two things against us: we are early, with no published customer testimonials yet, and several industry systems connect through Zapier rather than directly. Neither is buried — the integrations list names every "bridge for now" row, and the pricing page publishes every rate before you speak to anyone.

The fair test isn't which vendor writes the better guide. It's what each one produces when you point it at your own business — so make all of them do that, including us.
