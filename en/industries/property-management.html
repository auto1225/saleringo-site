Where the AI stops talking

Three questions
it will not answer.

Who pays. How much can be spent. What the tenancy allows. Each one is a person's decision under a specific agreement, and each one is the kind of answer that costs an agency a management contract when a machine improvises it. These limits ship enabled; they are not settings someone can switch off to close a works order faster.

Never decided Landlord or tenant liability

"Am I paying for this?" is recorded verbatim against the works order, with the tenancy attached, and queued for the property manager. No hint. No "usually it's the landlord". No reading the tenancy back as if that were an answer.

Hard ceiling Spend above the landlord's limit

Below the approval ceiling on that property it can raise a works order to make safe. At or above it, it makes safe within the ceiling and pages the manager for the rest. It never authorises the difference and never asks a contractor to invoice later.

Hard boundary No tenancy-law advice, ever

Deposits, notice, eviction, rent increases, right to repair. It captures the question word for word, says plainly that a person has to answer it, and routes the thread. It also never tells a tenant that a hazard is acceptable, in any wording.

STEP 01Make safeWater near electrics, gas, no heat in winter, a door that will not lock — your isolation wording is read before any detail is taken.
STEP 02Test the definitionThe report is checked against the emergency list in that property's management agreement. Tonight, or Monday — never a guess.
STEP 03Dispatch inside the limitThe approved contractor for that trade at that block, at or under the landlord's ceiling. Anything above it pages a person.
STEP 04Leave a timestamped trailReport time, make-safe time, dispatch time, landlord notification — on one works order, with the recording attached.

One landlord's rules never
reach another landlord's unit.

A managing agent's risk is not volume, it is cross-contamination: the wrong ceiling, the wrong contractor, the wrong emergency definition applied to the wrong door. Each property is set up as its own object inside one account.

01Its own emergency definition

What counts as an out-of-hours emergency is read from that property's management agreement. One landlord's "no heat is an emergency in winter" never becomes another's.

02Its own ceiling and its own contractors

A $250 limit on one block and a $600 limit on another are two different rules, and the approved plumber for Block A is not dispatched to Block C because he happened to be first on a list.

03A portfolio ends where that manager’s units end

A property manager opens the board and sees their units, their works orders, their escalations — not the whole agency's.

FAQ

Agencies like yours ask us

A tenant calls at 1 AM with water coming through a ceiling. What exactly happens?

Make-safe wording first, dispatch second. The AI reads your approved isolation script — stopcock, consumer unit, move out from under the ceiling — then checks the issue against the emergency definition in that property's management agreement. If it qualifies and the job sits inside the landlord's approval ceiling, it raises the works order to the approved contractor for that block and notifies the landlord. Every step carries a timestamp.

Will it decide whether the landlord or the tenant pays?

Never. Liability under a specific tenancy is a person's decision. It records the question against the works order, attaches the tenancy, and queues it for the property manager. It does not hint, it does not say "usually", and it does not read the tenancy back as advice.

What stops it spending the landlord's money?

The approval ceiling on that property. Below the ceiling it can raise a works order to make safe. At or above it, it makes safe within the ceiling and pages the property manager for the rest — it never authorises the difference itself, and it never asks a contractor to "just do it and invoice later".

Does it give tenancy advice — deposits, notice, rent increases?

No, in any wording. Deposits, notice periods, eviction, rent increases and right-to-repair questions all route to the property manager with the thread attached. The AI captures the question verbatim so the tenant does not have to ask it twice.

We manage for several landlords with different rules. Does that work?

That is the point of the pack. The emergency definition, the approval ceiling and the approved contractor list are held per property, not per agency. A caller about one unit never triggers another landlord's rules or another block's contractor.

Do you integrate with Arthur, Propertyware or Fixflo?

Not directly, and we will not pretend otherwise. Works orders reach them today through Zapier, a direct feed or CSV export, and founding-cohort members vote on the build order. A shipping month we might miss is not worth the sentence it takes to write.

Can I start with out-of-hours only?

That is how most agents begin. Forward the line after 5:30 PM and at weekends, read a week of transcripts, then widen the window once the answers sound like your office. Narrowing the window again is a change you make on your own phone system; nobody has to raise a ticket with us.

The log is the evidence

Most jurisdictions put a clock
on a repair. Very few agents
can prove when it started.

Housing law almost everywhere defines an emergency repair and attaches a response window to it. Hazard-response timescales in the United Kingdom under the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act and the Awaab's Law regime. The implied warranty of habitability and state repair timescales across the United States. Equivalent rules elsewhere. The duty is local and we do not claim one worldwide standard. What is the same everywhere is the question that gets asked afterwards: when was it reported, and what happened next?

A report logged at 01:15, a make-safe confirmed at 01:22 and a works order accepted at 01:34 is a record. A voicemail heard at nine is not. That is the part of this product a managing agent should actually be buying, and it is why the timestamps on this page are printed as prominently as the prices.

Two things this does not mean. It is not legal advice, and Saleringo does not assess compliance — your agency and its advisers do that. And the AI never tells a tenant that a hazard is acceptable, in any wording, however small the report sounds. It logs, it makes safe, it escalates.
